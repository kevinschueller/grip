Twelve weeks

Three gears, one habit. The dose changes. Showing up does not.

The movements

Progress by adding weight, not reps, once the top of the range feels like a 6 out of 10 effort. Two pounds is a real jump. If a joint complains rather than the tendon, change the angle before you change the plan.

What you are fixing

Not inflammation. A tendon that met more load than it could take, and now needs building back up.

The short version

The muscles that curl your wrist and close your fingers anchor onto a bony bump on the inside of your elbow. That shared anchor is the common flexor origin. When gripping, lifting and twisting outrun what it can take, the tendon gets sore to press, sore to load, and sore the day after a big session.

It is usually not inflamed, which is why rest alone stalls. Tendons answer to progressive load. You are not resting it better, you are teaching it to tolerate more.

Three rules that decide everything

01

Keep it at 3 out of 10 or less during exercise. A dull ache is allowed. Sharp is not.

02

No worse the next morning. If it is, you overdid it. Drop a set or a few pounds and continue.

03

Read it in weeks. The line should fall over two to three week blocks. Day to day noise means nothing.

Load management, concretely

  • Straps on pulls above half your usual weight.
  • Neutral grip instead of palm up. Hammer curls before barbell curls.
  • Cut pulling volume by about a third for two weeks.
  • Wear a counterforce strap just below the elbow for long gripping work.
  • Warm the forearm up before anything heavy. Two minutes is enough.
  • Do not stretch it hard. Tight is an irritated tendon, not a short muscle.

Stop and get it looked at if

  • You cannot fully straighten or bend the arm.
  • Ring and pinky fingers go numb, or you are dropping things.
  • The elbow is hot, red and swollen, or you have a fever.
  • Pain wakes you at night or climbs week after week.
  • It began with a pop and there is bruising.

Asked and answered

General education, not medical advice, and not a diagnosis. If it is not clearly better after four to six weeks of consistent loading, see a physical therapist or an orthopedist.
